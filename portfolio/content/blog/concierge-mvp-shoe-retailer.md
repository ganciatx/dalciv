Zappos didn't start with a warehouse full of inventory. Neither should you — at least not on day one.

## The concierge MVP approach

A concierge MVP means you manually fulfill the service your product would eventually automate. For a shoe retailer, that means:

1. Build a simple website showcasing shoes (photos from manufacturers)
2. When someone orders, you manually buy the shoes and ship them
3. Learn what people actually buy, return, and complain about

You're not trying to be profitable. You're trying to **learn**.

## What Zappos actually did

Nick Swinmurn couldn't get shoes in his size at a mall. Instead of building logistics infrastructure, he:

- Took photos of shoes at local stores
- Listed them online
- When orders came in, bought the shoes at retail and shipped them
- Lost money on every sale — intentionally

The goal was validating that people would buy shoes online at all. They did.

## When to use this pattern

Concierge MVPs work best when:

- The core question is "will people pay for this?" not "can we build this?"
- Manual fulfillment is possible (even if inefficient)
- You can run the experiment in 2–4 weeks
- The learning is worth more than the margin loss

## What to measure

| Metric | What it tells you |
|--------|-------------------|
| Orders per week | Demand exists |
| Return rate | Product-market fit quality |
| Customer emails | Unmet needs and pain points |
| Repeat purchases | Retention signal |

## When to stop the concierge phase

Move to automation when you've validated demand and understand the operational bottlenecks. If nobody orders after 4 weeks of real traffic, the concierge phase saved you months of engineering.

The shoes were never the point. The learning was.
